How to use this guide

You learn one thing every day for 45 minutes. After 4 weeks you will have covered every area of Carbon Clarity once. Then start again from Week 1 and go deeper. Each day's section in your calendar matches a section below.

The daily routine (7:00–7:45)

  1. Read (20 min). Open the reading for today's day and week. Skim the headings first, then read slowly.
  2. Understand (15 min). Read the key ideas in this guide. Answer the two questions in your own words, on paper.
  3. Write your 3 sentences (10 min). Add one row to the notes log at the end of this guide:
    • Today I learned…
    • It matters for Carbon Clarity because…
    • I could explain it to a fisher, a funder or a professor as…

Monday: Carbon markets & MRV

Goal: by Week 4 you can explain step by step how wood saved in a James Town smoker becomes a carbon credit and a mobile-money payment. MRV means Measurement, Reporting and Verification. It is the evidence buyers pay for.

Week 1: What a carbon credit is

Read: Carbon Brief Q&A on carbon offsets, the sections on what offsets are and on cookstoves.

Key ideas

  • One credit = one tonne of CO₂ kept out of the air (tCO₂e).
  • A stove project earns credits for the wood it saves compared with the old kiln (the baseline).
  • Only wood taken from forests faster than they regrow counts. That share is fNRB (fraction of non-renewable biomass).
  • The core calculation:

Emission reductions = wood saved (t) × fNRB × energy per tonne × CO₂ per unit of energy

Answer in your own words: Why does fNRB matter so much? What baseline would we use for Chorkor kilns?

Week 2: Gold Standard metered & measured method

Read: Gold Standard Methodology for Metered & Measured Energy Cooking Devices (MECD), the summary page and the first pages of the PDF.

Key ideas

  • Credits are based on energy measured at the device (meters, sensors), not on surveys or estimates.
  • This is why our IoT sensors and the Data Vault matter: they could make Carbon Clarity credits measured, not guessed.
  • It was written mainly for electric and gas stoves. Ask how far it could fit biomass fish smokers.

Answer: What would we need to measure on each smoker every day? Who checks the data?

Week 3: Verra VM0050

Read: Verra VM0050, Energy Efficiency and Fuel Switch Measures in Cookstoves, the overview.

Key ideas

  • Verra's newer cookstove method, made stricter after over-crediting criticism.
  • It uses more conservative fNRB values and requires checking that stoves are actually used.
  • Compare it with Gold Standard: who measures, how often, and how conservative.

Answer: Name two ways VM0050 is stricter than older methods.

Week 4: CLEAR, the newest approach

Read: CLEAR Methodology, Clean Cooking Alliance. For background, read the MECS blog on metered credits.

Key ideas

  • CLEAR was built to fix the over-crediting problems in older cookstove methods.
  • It relies on measured fuel use and on conservative, up-to-date defaults.
  • High-integrity credits sell for more. Integrity is a selling point for Carbon Clarity, not a cost.

Answer: If a buyer asked "How do I know your credits are real?", what would you say in 3 sentences?

Tuesday: Research methods & data

Goal: you can defend every number in the working paper and the pilot, and answer "How do you know?" from any professor.

Week 1: Designing a good survey

Read: Pew Research Center, Writing Survey Questions.

Key ideas

  • Ask one thing per question. Avoid leading words ("Don't you agree the new stove is better?").
  • Pilot the questionnaire with 3–5 processors before the real survey, in Ga or Twi.
  • Record fuel use, cost, smoke exposure and income the same way at baseline and after the pilot. Otherwise you can't compare.

Answer: Rewrite one question from our baseline survey so it is clearer and not leading.

Week 2: Thematic analysis of interviews

Read: Braun & Clarke (2006), Using thematic analysis in psychology, the six phases.

Key ideas

  • Six phases: get familiar, code, find themes, review themes, name them, write up.
  • A theme is a pattern across many interviews, not one quote you like.
  • Keep an audit trail (codebook, dates, who coded) so a reviewer can trust the analysis.

Answer: What themes do you expect from the James Town interviews? How will you check you are not just confirming what you expect?

Week 3: Basic statistics in R

Read: R for Data Science (2nd ed.), chapters "Whole game" and "Data visualization". Install R and RStudio first (free).

Key ideas

  • Mean and median describe the middle. Standard deviation describes the spread.
  • A paired t-test compares the same processors before and after, for example kg of wood per batch on the Chorkor versus the Ahotor.
  • With a small pilot, report the size of the difference with a confidence interval, not just "significant".

Answer: If 10 processors used 20% less wood, what else would you need to know before claiming the stove works?

Week 4: Writing for peer review

Read: Mensh & Kording (2017), Ten simple rules for structuring papers.

Key ideas

  • One central message per paper. Every section serves it.
  • Each paragraph starts with its point. Each figure tells one story.
  • Reviewers look first at the title, abstract and figures. Make them strong.

Answer: Write the central message of the working paper in one sentence of under 25 words.

Wednesday: Stove technology & measurement

Goal: you can explain why the Ahotor produces less smoke than the Chorkor, and how we will prove it with numbers.

Week 1: How fire and smoke work

Read: Peace Corps Improved Cookstoves Handbook, the chapter on combustion and stove design principles.

Key ideas

  • Clean burning needs three things: enough heat, enough air, and enough time for gases to burn (the "three Ts": temperature, turbulence, time).
  • Smoke is unburnt fuel. Less smoke usually means less wood wasted.
  • In fish smoking, fat dripping onto the fire makes the most harmful smoke (PAHs, cancer-causing compounds). The Ahotor and FAO-Thiaroye (FTT) designs separate the fire from the dripping fat.

Answer: Draw the Chorkor and the Ahotor from the side. Mark where the fire, the fish and the dripping fat are.

Week 2: Testing stoves to a standard

Read: the ISO 19867-1:2018 summary page, then reread your copy of L.I. 2454.

Key ideas

  • ISO 19867-1 is the international lab test for cookstoves: efficiency, emissions, safety and durability, scored in tiers.
  • Ghana's L.I. 2454 uses it: a safety score of at least 85, efficiency of at least 25% for wood and above 35% for charcoal, plus labelling.
  • Lab results are often better than real use. That's why we also measure in the field at James Town.

Answer: Does L.I. 2454 clearly cover commercial fish smokers? What would you ask the Energy Commission?

Week 3: Measuring smoke exposure

Read: Weyant et al. (2022), Environmental Health Perspectives, on PM₂.₅ and CO exposure.

Key ideas

  • PM₂.₅ = tiny particles that go deep into the lungs. CO = carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas.
  • Personal monitors worn by the processor show what she actually breathes, not just what the kiln gives off.
  • Measure the same women before and after the switch, at the same tasks, for 24–48 hours.

Answer: Why is a personal monitor better evidence than one sensor on the kiln?

Week 4: Sensors, IoT and audit-ready data

Read: reread the Gold Standard MECD page from Monday Week 2, then open the Data Vault app and list every field it records.

Key ideas

  • Sensors can record temperature, run time and fuel weight automatically, with a timestamp.
  • "Audit-ready" means: raw data kept, each device has an ID, changes logged, and calibration recorded.
  • Good data serves three needs at once: the paper (Phase 1), the stove design (Phase 2) and carbon credits (Phase 3).

Answer: What three things must the Data Vault record for a verifier to trust it?

Thursday: Funding & running the venture

Goal: you can ask for money with confidence and show exactly how it will be used and repaid in impact.

Week 1: Writing a winning grant

Read: Bourne & Chalupa (2006), Ten simple rules for getting grants.

Key ideas

  • Read the funder's goals first. Write to their goals using your evidence.
  • Structure: problem → what you will do → why you → budget → measurable results.
  • Ask a colleague to read it as a reviewer before you submit.

Answer: Write a 3-sentence problem statement for Carbon Clarity that a funder would remember.

Week 2: A simple financial model

Read: browse the free courses at Acumen Academy on social enterprise finance and business models.

Key ideas

  • Know three numbers: cost per stove installed, cost per processor per year to run MRV, and expected credits per stove per year.
  • Revenue per stove = credits × price per credit. Then subtract verification costs and the processors' benefit share.
  • Test a low, middle and high carbon price. Never plan only for the best price.

Answer: List the costs of the comparative pilot. Which one is most uncertain?

Week 3: Pitching

Read: Y Combinator Startup Library, search "how to pitch".

Key ideas

  • Within 30 seconds say: the problem, who has it, your solution, and the proof so far.
  • Use one striking number (for example, hours of smoke exposure per day).
  • End with a clear ask: money, a partner or an introduction.

Answer: Write and time a 60-second pitch. Record it on Saturday.

Week 4: Partnerships & agreements

Read: reread any agreement or MoU you have (CSIR-FRI, Ashesi, the processors' association), with this checklist in mind.

Key ideas

  • Every agreement should say: who does what, who owns the data, who owns the carbon credits, and how money is shared.
  • Benefit-sharing by mobile money must be written down before credits are sold, and agreed with the women themselves.
  • Get legal advice before signing anything about carbon rights.

Answer: Who should own the carbon credits from a stove: the processor, Carbon Clarity or the funder? Why?

Friday: Carbon markets & policy

Goal: you can answer tough critics of cookstove credits, and explain how Ghana's rules shape Carbon Clarity.

Week 1: What makes a credit high quality

Read: ICVCM Core Carbon Principles.

Key ideas

  • The ten principles include: additionality (it would not happen without carbon money), permanence, robust quantification (no over-counting), no double counting, and sustainable development benefits.
  • Credits that pass get a "CCP" label and usually sell for a higher price.

Answer: Is our stove project additional? Argue both sides in two sentences each.

Week 2: The over-crediting critique

Read: Gill-Wiehl, Kammen & Haya (2024), Nature Sustainability, the abstract and figures. A plain summary is on Phys.org.

Key ideas

  • The study found cookstove projects claimed about 9 times more reductions than they really achieved.
  • Main causes: fNRB set too high, stove use overestimated, and old stoves still used alongside new ones (stacking).
  • Our answer: measure actual fuel use, use conservative fNRB, and check whether the Chorkor is still used.

Answer: A professor says "cookstove credits are a scam". Write your 3-sentence reply.

Week 3: Article 6 and Ghana's carbon market

Read: KliK Foundation, first ITMO transfer Switzerland–Ghana, and GNA, Ghana's carbon market to generate over $1 billion by 2030.

Key ideas

  • Article 6 of the Paris Agreement lets countries trade emission reductions (ITMOs).
  • Ghana was among the first to transfer Article 6 credits, from a cookstove project, to Switzerland.
  • Ghana requires carbon projects to be authorised and registered nationally, and the government takes a share. Find out what applies to us.

Answer: What is a "corresponding adjustment", and why does Ghana care about it?

Week 4: Ghana's clean cooking policy & L.I. 2454

Read: your copy of L.I. 2454 and the policy section (2.5) of the working paper.

Key ideas

  • L.I. 2454 sets minimum stove performance and labels so that poor-quality stoves are kept off the market.
  • Show that the stove we pilot meets it, or explain clearly why fish smokers need their own standard.
  • Policy is an opportunity: our data could help the Energy Commission set a standard for fish smokers.

Answer: In 3 sentences, explain to a policymaker why fish smokers need attention in clean cooking policy.

Saturday practice: explain it out loud (9:00–10:00)

This is the session that builds confidence. You don't learn anything new. You prove to yourself that you can explain what you learned.

  1. Re-read (10 min). Read this week's five notes in the log below.
  2. Pick the hardest topic (5 min). Choose the one you feel least sure about.
  3. Explain it in 2 minutes, without notes (15 min). Record it on your phone. Listen back. Do it again, better.
  4. Write one plain paragraph (10 min) that a fish processor in James Town would understand. No jargon.
  5. Answer 3 hard questions (20 min) from the bank below, out loud, as if a provost or funder asked you.

Once a month, explain one topic to your co-author or an intern and ask them to challenge you.

Hard questions bank

  • How do you know the new stove actually saves wood, and isn't just used on good days?
  • Cookstove credits have been over-credited by about 9 times. Why should anyone trust yours?
  • Who owns the carbon credits, and how much reaches the women?
  • Does the Ahotor meet L.I. 2454? If not, why pilot it?
  • What happens if processors go back to the Chorkor after the study ends?
  • Your sample is small. Why should we believe your results?
  • What does your fNRB value come from?
  • How is your stove design different from the Ahotor and the FTT?
  • What does it cost per tonne of CO₂ reduced?
  • How will the sensor data stay secure and honest?
  • Why are you the right team to do this?
  • What's the single most important finding of your baseline study?

  • What is the one thing I most want to understand better next week?

Glossary of key terms

Term Plain meaning
Additionality The reductions would not happen without carbon money
Article 6 Part of the Paris Agreement that lets countries trade emission reductions
Baseline What would happen without the project, e.g. the Chorkor kiln
CCP Core Carbon Principles, the ICVCM quality label for credits
CO Carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas from incomplete burning
Corresponding adjustment The selling country removes the credits from its own climate target so they aren't counted twice
fNRB Share of wood taken faster than forests regrow; only this share earns credits
ISO 19867-1 International lab test for cookstove performance
ITMO Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcome, a credit traded under Article 6
L.I. 2454 Ghana's legal standard and labelling rules for cookstoves
MRV Measurement, Reporting and Verification
PAHs Cancer-causing compounds formed when fat burns in smoke
PM₂.₅ Tiny smoke particles that go deep into the lungs
Stacking Using the old and new stoves side by side
tCO₂e One tonne of CO₂ or its equivalent: one carbon credit
Verification An independent auditor checks the data before credits are issued
