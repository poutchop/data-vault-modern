The problem

Behind every smoked fish in Ghana is a woman working inside the smoke.

Smoked fish is a staple, and the women who produce it carry the cost in their health, their fuel bills and their forests.

≈80%of the fish landed in Ghana is smoked (SNV)
GHS 700for one crate of raw fish, paid up front
GHS 10per day to rent a smoker; a new one costs GHS 300

The cost to her health

  • Burning eyes and chronic conjunctivitis
  • Persistent coughing and dizziness
  • Burns on fingers and forearms
  • Clinics tell her to stop smoking fish. It is her only livelihood.

The cost to her income

  • Many women smoke on commission, with no capital of their own.
  • Landlords collect daily rent on the smokers.
  • Firewood is a large part of what she spends every day.
  • Oily mackerel drips fat onto the embers, which flares into black soot, yet buyers want the golden, smoky result.
The gap. Carbon programmes reward cleaner cooking, but they often pay the project developer, not the woman standing at the fire. Carbon Clarity is built so the credit income reaches her.
The solution

Three projects under one umbrella

Each one solves a different part of the problem. Together they turn cleaner smoking into verified credits, and credits into income for the women.

Project 1 · The stove

An improved smoked-fish cookstove

Designed for the women who use it, and tested head to head against the traditional kiln.

  • Comparative study of traditional and improved stoves
  • Results become the blueprint for the next-phase design
  • Less firewood, less smoke, same golden fish
Project 2 · The data

IoT sensors that auditors can trust

Field sensors capture cooking and fuel data automatically, so the claims behind each credit are measured, not estimated.

  • 12-unit sensor pilot (10 active, 2 spare)
  • Data collected to meet the evidence requirements of international carbon standards and auditors
  • Cloud storage and cellular connectivity built in
Project 3 · The money

A women-first benefit-sharing model

Carbon-credit income is designed to flow to the women directly, by mobile money, so it covers the things she decides it should.

  • Share size and payout rules co-designed with the women
  • Paid direct to her phone, no middlemen
  • Model in development
How they connect

From a better stove to money in her hand

Step 1Improved stove
Step 2Less firewood burned
Step 3Sensors verify the saving
Step 4Credits are issued and sold
Step 5Her share is paid to her phone

Less firewood also means less pressure on trees and lower carbon emissions. Our target is for women to spend up to 50% less on firewood, to be confirmed by the comparative study. That money stays with her.

Next milestone

The comparative study

We have not run it yet. A traditional Chorkor kiln that processors already use will be tested side by side with a modified Ahotor oven from CSIR-FRI: same site, same processor, same fish, same fuel. It is the evidence everything else depends on.

Arm A · Baseline

Traditional Chorkor kiln

The existing kiln at the site, owned or rented by the processor, so nothing is bought. Stacked mesh trays sit directly over an open wood fire, and fat drips onto the flames.

Arm B · Improved

Modified Ahotor oven

Designed by the CSIR-Food Research Institute and built in Ghana. An enclosed combustion chamber and a fat-collecting tray keep drippings off the fire. Runs on wood, charcoal or LPG. This is the one oven we buy for the pilot.

Arm C · Optional

Retrofitted Chorkor

An existing Chorkor fitted with a fat-collector tray. It tests whether a low-cost upgrade to a kiln women already rent gets most of the benefit.

Evidence so far

We started on the ground, in James Town

In October 2026 the team visited the James Town fish landing site in Accra and spoke with artisanal women fish processors. The baseline work is done; it comes before any stove is built.

What the October fieldwork produced

  • Field visit and interviews with women fish processors
  • A map of the five tiers of the smoking value chain
  • A working paper draft on artisanal fish smoking in James Town
  • Observations of how kilns burn, what fuels they use and why soot forms

Who is in the system

  • The women smokers: commission and independent processors, plus adolescent assistants
  • Enablers: smoker landlords, fuelwood haulers, fish financiers
  • Informal inputs: cardboard scavengers, kiln masons, urban consumers
  • Governance and research: clinics, NAFPTA, Fisheries Commission, CSIR-FRI, FDA Ghana
Roadmap

18 months, from baseline to a working pilot

September 2026 to February 2028, in six quarters. Timing is indicative and will follow what the study finds.

  1. October 2026

    Listen first Done

    James Town field visit, interviews with women processors and the stakeholder map.

  2. November – December 2026

    Survey and working paper Now

    Surveys of 25–30 women processors, plus interviews with fuelwood suppliers and landlords. A working paper on fish smoking in James Town.

  3. 2027

    Comparative study and sensor build Planned

    Traditional kiln versus improved stove, with sensors on both. Co-design of the next-phase stove with the women. Lab calibration of the sensing kit.

  4. Mid 2027 onward

    Benefit-sharing model tested Planned

    Mobile-money micro-incentives trialled with women taking part, and the payout rules refined with them.

  5. Late 2027 – February 2028

    Pilot results and publications Planned

    Verified data from the 12-unit sensor pilot, two open-access journal papers and a model other programmes can reuse.

Straight answers

What is proven, and what is still to prove

Established

  • The conditions at James Town: smoke exposure, fuel costs and who is involved
  • The research team and a costed 18-month plan
  • Strong buyer demand for golden, well-smoked fish

Still to prove

  • How much firewood and money the improved stove actually saves
  • Whether sensor data meets a given carbon standard’s method
  • The size of the women’s share, which we will set with them and sponsors
